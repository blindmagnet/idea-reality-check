# The Self-Audit

This is the last thing you do before delivering, and it exists because the failure mode this skill
guards against is not stupidity — it is agreeableness. The pull toward encouragement is strong,
persistent, and mostly invisible from the inside. A written checklist catches what judgement in
the moment does not.

Read the finished report against every item below. Where something fails, fix the report rather
than noting the exception.

## The twelve softenings

**1. The buried conclusion.**
Is the real finding in the first paragraph, or does it arrive on page four after a warm-up? Open
the report and read only the first three sentences: do they contain the actual verdict? If the
finding is "this cannot be paid for in this market", that belongs at the top, not after a
respectful tour of the idea's strengths.

**2. The hedged blocker.**
Search the report for anything described as a "challenge", "consideration", "hurdle", or
"something to keep in mind" that is in fact a condition ending the idea in its current form. A
blocker written as a risk is the single most consequential softening this skill can commit,
because the founder will read it as manageable and proceed.

**3. The scorecard of sevens.**
If the report assigns scores and they cluster in a comfortable middle, they are decoration. Real
assessments are lumpy: some dimensions are fine and one is catastrophic. A row of 6s and 7s means
no dimension was actually assessed.

**4. The laundered assumption.**
Trace every number in the conclusion back to where it entered the report. Did its tag survive? A
conversion rate that appears as `[assumed]` in the inputs table and as a plain fact in the
summary has been laundered, and the conclusion is now resting on air.

**5. False balance.**
Are strengths and weaknesses presented at equal length regardless of their actual weight? If one
weakness is fatal and four strengths are cosmetic, a balanced-looking layout misrepresents the
situation. Proportion should follow importance, not symmetry.

**6. The vanishing comparison.**
Does the report compare the idea to doing nothing, or to the founder's real alternative? "It could
make $900/month" reads as success until placed next to what those hours currently earn. If the
opportunity-cost comparison is missing or softly worded, the report is flattering by omission.

**7. The unnamed customer.**
Does the report ever describe the customer as "anyone who", "businesses", "people who want", or
"users"? If the audit accepted a customer definition that vague, phase 1 did not actually happen
and the market sizing downstream is fiction.

**8. Top-down survival.**
Search for any market-size figure that was not built bottom-up from countable entities. Even one
inherited "$X billion market" number will do the founder's thinking for them, in the wrong
direction.

**9. The soft experiment.**
Does the proposed experiment have a budget, a deadline, and a pass/fail number set in advance? "Talk
to some potential customers" is not an experiment — it is a plan to gather encouragement. And does
it test the *top-ranked* assumption, or a more comfortable one further down the list?

**10. Excitement-adjusted verdict.**
Ask directly: if this founder had presented the same idea flatly, or said they were already
sceptical, would the verdict be the same? If the answer is no, the verdict was set by their
enthusiasm rather than by the evidence, and it needs rewriting.

**11. The deferred no.**
Does the report avoid a conclusion by suggesting more research, more thinking, or a later
decision, when the evidence is actually sufficient to decide now? Deferral feels kind and costs the
founder months.

**12. Cheerful arithmetic.**
Re-read the model inputs. Would a sceptic accept each one? Conversion at the top of the plausible
range, churn at the bottom, founder time at zero, no ramp period — each individually defensible,
compounding into a fantasy. Check them as a set, not one at a time.

## The opposite failure

Harshness is not rigour, and it is the more self-satisfied of the two errors.

**Contrarianism without evidence.** "Most startups fail", "the market is crowded", "big players
will crush you" are moods, not findings. Every negative claim needs a reason specific to this
idea, in this market, at this price.

**Unfalsifiable negativity.** If the report offers no conditions under which the idea would be
viable, it is not an analysis — it is a verdict wearing analysis as a costume. Section 7 exists
precisely to prevent this.

**Dismissing the insight along with the model.** Founders are usually right about the problem and
wrong about the business. A report that rejects the whole idea without examining whether the
insight survives in a different form has done half the job, and the missing half is often the
valuable one.

**STOP without three facts.** If STOP cannot be supported with three concrete, specific,
sourced facts, the honest verdict is TEST.

## Two questions that catch most of it

Before sending, answer these honestly:

> **If this founder builds this and it fails in eighteen months, what will the reason be — and does
> the report say it, in those words, near the top?**

If the reason is in the report but softened, or present but buried, fix that now. This is the
question the entire skill is built around.

> **Would I hand this report to the founder's closest friend, and would they say it was fair?**

Fair, not kind and not brutal. A good audit is one the founder can disagree with specifically, act
on immediately, and re-read in a year without wincing at either its flattery or its cruelty.
