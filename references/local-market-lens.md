# The Local Market Lens

The same idea is a company in one country and an impossibility in another, and the difference is
rarely the idea. This is the phase that most analyses skip — because the analyst and the framework
both come from somewhere with functioning payment rails, cheap ads, and permissive regulation —
and it is the phase that most often decides the outcome.

Work through all eight dimensions. Most will be unremarkable; you are looking for the one or two
that are not.

## Two separate geographies

Before anything else, establish both, because they are frequently different and the difference is
the whole analysis:

- **Where the founder is** — determines the cost base, what they can legally register and operate,
  which payment rails they can *receive* through, which platforms will accept them, what tax and
  banking friction exists, and how many hours of their life a given income represents.
- **Where the customers are** — determines purchasing power, the price anchor, the channels, the
  regulation, and how the customer expects to pay.

A developer in a sanctioned or banking-restricted country selling to the US has a *receiving*
problem the idea itself does not create. A founder in a high-cost country selling locally has a
*price floor* problem. Both are real; they are not the same problem and they do not have the same
solutions.

## 1. Payments and money movement

The most common silent killer. An idea can be perfect and simply have no way to get paid.

- Can the customer pay at all, in the way the model requires? Card penetration, bank transfer
  norms, cash-on-delivery expectations, mobile money, local wallets.
- **Does recurring billing exist?** This is the one to check first for any subscription model.
  Many markets have no card-on-file recurring rail available to a small business, which converts a
  subscription into a manual monthly invoice — a different business with a different churn profile
  and a different amount of the founder's time.
- Can the founder *receive*? Stripe, Paddle, PayPal, and the app stores are unavailable in a
  number of countries, and availability to a company differs from availability to an individual.
- What do the fees actually cost? Gateway percentage, fixed per-transaction fee, currency
  conversion spread, withdrawal fee, and the cost of getting money out of the platform and into a
  usable account. On a low-price product these can consume a fifth of revenue.
- Refunds, chargebacks, and holding periods — a platform holding funds for 90 days changes the
  cash-flow model entirely for a bootstrapped founder.
- Currency risk: costs in one currency and revenue in another, in a market with a volatile rate,
  is a business decision disguised as an accounting detail.

**If the payment rail the model needs does not exist, that is a blocker, not a risk.** Say so, then
look for the routed version — annual invoice instead of monthly card, local gateway instead of
international, a partner or entity abroad, a different price point that suits the available rail.

## 2. Purchasing power and price anchoring

A price is only meaningful relative to local incomes and local comparables.

- What does the target customer actually earn, or what is the business's revenue? A $50/month tool
  is trivial in one market and a serious line item in another.
- What is the local anchor for this category? Founders routinely price against what they saw a
  Silicon Valley company charge, which sets an expectation the local customer has never seen.
- Is this a market where people pay for software, or one where the assumption is that software is
  free and value lives in hardware, service, or human labour? This is cultural and it is stubborn.
- What is the labour comparison? If hiring a person to do this manually costs less than the
  software, the software must justify itself against that, and often cannot.
- Is piracy or sharing of paid accounts normal and unpoliced? It changes the effective price.

## 3. Regulation, licensing, and legal form

- Does this activity require a licence, permit, or professional qualification? Health, finance,
  education, food, transport, and legal services are regulated nearly everywhere, at wildly
  varying strictness.
- Can the founder legally operate as an individual, or is a registered entity required — and what
  does registering cost in money, time, and ongoing obligation?
- Data protection: what applies, and what does it require in practice? Handling personal or health
  data raises the compliance floor sharply.
- Sector-specific rules: advertising restrictions, content licensing, platform rules that are
  stricter than the law, import/export controls.
- Sanctions and cross-border restrictions, in both directions — what the founder's country
  restricts, and what other countries restrict about the founder's country. This affects which
  suppliers, APIs, cloud providers, and payment processors are usable, and it can change without
  warning.
- Tax: VAT/GST registration thresholds, whether it must be charged to local consumers, and what
  compliance costs in time.

## 4. Infrastructure and platform access

- Connectivity, device mix, mobile-versus-desktop reality of the target user.
- Logistics and delivery, if anything physical moves: coverage, cost, reliability, returns.
- Are the technical dependencies available? Cloud regions, API access, model providers, map data,
  SMS gateways, identity verification services — several of these are geographically restricted,
  and a dependency that requires a foreign card to purchase is a dependency the founder may not
  have.
- Platform access: can the founder publish to the app stores, run ads on the major networks, accept
  the major payment methods? Account restrictions by country are common and rarely appealable.
- Internet access stability, filtering, or shutdown risk where relevant — a business that stops
  earning during an outage needs that in the model, not as a footnote.

## 5. Distribution channels that actually exist here

Channels are not universal. The list of "growth channels" in most startup writing describes one
country.

- Which platforms does this audience actually use? The globally dominant network may be irrelevant
  or inaccessible locally.
- What does paid acquisition cost here, if it is available at all? Some markets have cheap ads and
  no card to buy them with.
- Does search-engine marketing work, or does discovery happen inside a messaging app, a
  marketplace, or a trade association?
- Are there local marketplaces, aggregators, directories, or intermediaries that own the demand?
  In many markets the route to the customer is *through* an incumbent platform, which changes the
  economics and the power balance entirely.
- Do offline channels dominate — trade fairs, associations, distributors, personal networks,
  religious or community institutions? In much of the world B2B sales are relationship-led and a
  cold email campaign is not a channel.
- Language: does this require content, support, and sales in the local language, and who does
  that?

## 6. Cultural and behavioural defaults

- Trust: will this audience pay before receiving? Prepayment resistance is a well-documented reason
  cash-on-delivery dominates certain markets, and it changes working capital needs.
- Attitude to subscriptions versus one-time purchase.
- Willingness to put data, documents, or money into an unknown online service.
- Purchase decision structure: individual, family, or committee.
- How complaints, refunds, and reputation work — in some markets a single public complaint in a
  community channel is fatal, in others reviews are ignored.
- Seasonality: religious calendars, fiscal years, harvest cycles, holiday periods that pause
  business entirely for weeks.

## 7. Cost base and operating reality

- What does it cost to run this, here? Hosting, tools, services, contractor rates, office if
  needed. A founder in a low-cost country has a genuine structural advantage when selling abroad —
  this is worth stating explicitly when true, because it is the strongest card in the hand.
- Can required tools be paid for? A subscription requiring an international card is a real
  obstacle in several markets and is worth checking rather than assuming.
- Inflation and currency instability: are costs or prices going to move materially within the
  model's horizon? If so, model in local currency and state the assumption.
- Hiring: if this needs a second person, do they exist locally, at what cost, and can they be paid
  legally and easily?

## 8. The ceiling

Where does this stop, from here?

- Can it scale beyond the local market, or is it structurally local?
- Would growth require an entity, bank account, or presence abroad — and is that achievable for
  this founder?
- Is the local market large enough on its own to reach the founder's goal? If the whole national
  market at full penetration is below their target income, the idea needs to be international from
  day one, and that changes everything about the plan.
- Does the founder's location limit funding, partnership, or acquisition options, if those are
  part of the goal?

## Blockers versus risks

Be strict about this distinction, because blurring it is the most consequential softening this
skill can commit.

A **risk** reduces the probability of success and can be mitigated, insured against, or accepted.

A **blocker** ends the idea *in its current form*. No recurring-payment rail for a subscription
business. A licence the founder cannot obtain. A required supplier that will not serve this
country. A platform that does not accept accounts from here.

State blockers plainly and early in the report — not buried in a risk list — and always pair them
with the routed alternative if one exists:

> **Blocker:** the model requires monthly card billing, which is not available to an unregistered
> individual in this market. `[sourced]`
>
> **Routes around it:** annual prepayment by bank transfer (changes the sales motion and the
> cash-flow profile); sell through a local marketplace that handles billing (loses margin and the
> customer relationship); register an entity abroad (cost, time, and legal exposure to assess).

A founder who learns about a blocker in month one can redesign. One who learns in month nine has
spent the money.

## Locale annexes

If `references/locales/<market>.md` exists for the founder's market, read it — it holds specifics
that generic reasoning will get wrong. If none exists, reason from this lens, verify what you can
with local-language search, and state clearly which local facts you could not verify. An honest
"I could not confirm whether recurring billing is available to individuals here — check this
first" is worth far more than a confident guess.

If in the course of an audit you establish solid local facts for a market with no annex, that is
exactly what belongs in a new one. See `CONTRIBUTING.md`.
