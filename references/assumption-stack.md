# The Assumption Stack

Every idea is a tower of beliefs. The founder usually sees the top brick — the product — and not
the twelve underneath it holding it up. Most ideas do not fail at the product. They fail because
one of the load-bearing bricks was never checked.

The purpose of this phase is to lay the bricks out on the table, named, so the audit can spend its
effort on the two or three that actually decide the outcome instead of spreading attention evenly
across everything.

## The seven categories

Work through each. For most ideas you will find between eight and twenty assumptions; more than
twenty usually means you are listing implementation details rather than beliefs.

### 1. Problem assumptions
*That the problem exists, and that it hurts.*

- The target person actually experiences this problem, at the frequency claimed
- They are aware they have it (many real problems are invisible to their owners)
- It is painful enough to change behaviour — not merely mildly annoying
- It recurs, rather than happening once (one-off problems make one-off revenue)
- It is their problem to solve, not their employer's, their landlord's, or their government's

The sharpest test: **what do they do about it today?** A problem with no workaround is usually not
a problem, it is an inconvenience. A problem with an elaborate manual workaround — spreadsheets,
WhatsApp groups, a person hired to do it — is a real one, and the workaround is the competitor.

### 2. Customer assumptions
*That a specific, findable group of people has this problem.*

- The customer can be described without the word "anyone" or "everyone"
- There are enough of them, reachable, in the served geography
- The person with the problem, the person who decides, and the person who pays are the same
  person — or, if not, all three are accounted for
- They are not structurally unable to buy (no budget authority, no card, no procurement path)

The buyer/user split kills more B2B ideas than any competitor does. Ask explicitly: who signs?

### 3. Solution assumptions
*That this particular thing solves it.*

- The solution removes the pain rather than relocating it
- It is meaningfully better than the current workaround — not 10% better; switching costs eat 10%
- It works without requiring the customer to change several other habits at once
- The quality required is achievable by this team, at this cost, in this time

### 4. Willingness-to-pay assumptions
*That they will hand over money, at the price required.*

This is the assumption most often skipped and most often fatal.

- They pay for this category of thing today, in some form
- The price needed for viability is a price they would accept
- The value is legible **before** purchase, not only after — unprovable value does not sell
- The budget exists and is not already allocated elsewhere
- Nobody gives an acceptable version away free

"People said they would pay" is not evidence. Stated intent runs far ahead of behaviour. Only
pre-payment, a deposit, or a signed commitment counts as evidence here.

### 5. Distribution assumptions
*That the founder can reach these people repeatedly, affordably.*

Ideas rarely die from a bad product. They die from having no route to the customer.

- A specific channel exists where these people are, and it is usable from this market
- That channel is affordable at the required volume — CAC must be a fraction of lifetime value
- It does not saturate the moment the founder's own network is exhausted
- The founder can actually execute it (a channel requiring daily video is not a channel for
  someone who will not make daily video)
- It does not depend on a single platform that can revoke access

"We'll use social media and word of mouth" is the absence of a distribution plan.

### 6. Feasibility and operations assumptions
*That it can be built and then run.*

- Technically achievable with available skills, budget, and time
- Required data, APIs, suppliers, licences, or integrations are obtainable **in this market**
- The ongoing work does not scale linearly with customers, unless the price reflects that
- It survives the founder taking a week off
- Legal and regulatory permission exists, and is affordable

### 7. Economic assumptions
*That the arithmetic closes.*

- Revenue per customer exceeds cost to serve, after payment fees and refunds
- Acquisition cost is recovered well inside the customer's lifetime
- Fixed costs are covered at a volume that is actually reachable
- The founder can survive the time to break-even
- It beats the founder's opportunity cost — the alternative use of the same hours

## Ranking: which assumptions matter

Score each assumption on two axes, 1–5.

**Load-bearing weight** — if this is false, what happens?

| Score | Consequence |
|---|---|
| 5 | The idea is over; nothing survives |
| 4 | The business model must change fundamentally |
| 3 | Substantially worse, still possible |
| 2 | A manageable problem |
| 1 | A detail |

**Uncertainty** — how confident are we, right now, with evidence?

| Score | State of evidence |
|---|---|
| 5 | Pure speculation, nothing either way |
| 4 | Plausible, unverified |
| 3 | Weak indirect evidence |
| 2 | Good evidence, some gaps |
| 1 | Verified |

Multiply. Sort descending. The top two or three are the **critical assumptions**, and they get the
research, the arithmetic, and the experiment. Anything scoring below about 9 can be noted and set
aside; spending the audit's attention there is how reports become long and useless.

A useful sanity check on the ranking: if the top-ranked assumption turned out to be true, would
you be confident in the idea? If not, something more important is missing from the list.

## Presenting the stack

Keep it to a table the founder can scan. Long prose here dilutes the signal.

| # | Assumption | Type | Weight | Uncertainty | Score | Current evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Clinics pay monthly rather than buy once | Willingness to pay | 5 | 5 | 25 | None — no comparable found `[assumed]` |
| 2 | Recurring card billing is available to a sole trader here | Feasibility | 5 | 4 | 20 | Provider docs indicate no `[sourced]` |
| 3 | There are ≥400 clinics of this size nationally | Customer | 4 | 3 | 12 | Association directory lists 512 `[sourced]` |

Then state, in one sentence, what the audit is really about:

> This idea stands or falls on whether clinics treat it as a subscription. Everything else is
> secondary, and if that one belief is wrong, nothing below it matters.

That sentence is the most valuable line in the report. It tells the founder where to spend their
next month.

## A recurring trap

**Confusing hard with important.** Founders fixate on the technically difficult assumption because
it is the one they can picture. The difficult one is often well understood and low risk; the
dangerous one is usually the boring commercial belief nobody examined. Rank by weight ×
uncertainty, not by how interesting the problem is.
