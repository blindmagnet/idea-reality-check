# The Verdict and the Report

The report exists to be argued with. Every number carries its epistemic tag, every conclusion
traces back to something, and the final section tells the founder exactly what evidence would
overturn the verdict. A report that can only be accepted or rejected wholesale has failed.

## The six verdicts

Choose by criteria, not by feel. These are not a severity ladder — REFRAME is not "worse than"
NOT-AS-A-BUSINESS, and TEST is not a milder STOP. They answer different questions, so pick by
asking, in this order:

1. **Is there a blocker with no viable route around it?** → STOP.
2. **Does the insight survive in a different business model than the one described?** → REFRAME.
   This question comes before the others because a REFRAME re-opens an idea the remaining grades
   would close. Ask it even when the described version looks dead.
3. **Do the economics fail against the founder's goal at every reachable volume, in every form?**
   → NOT-AS-A-BUSINESS.
4. **Do they work only for a specific slice of the described market?** → NARROW.
5. **Is everything else sound but the top-ranked assumption unverified?** → TEST.
6. **Is the top assumption verified and the rest sound?** → BUILD.

If two genuinely apply, name both in the report and say why you chose the one you did. That
paragraph is often more useful than the grade itself, because it tells the founder which fact
would move them from one to the other.

**BUILD** — the evidence supports it now.
Demand is demonstrated (people pay for this or its substitute today), the economics close at
achievable volumes, no blockers in the local market, and the top-ranked assumptions are verified
rather than assumed. Rare. If more than roughly one audit in five reaches BUILD, the bar is set
wrong.

Note what this implies: BUILD requires *verified* assumptions, and desk research alone usually
cannot verify a willingness-to-pay assumption — only a real customer handing over real money can.
So a first-pass audit will rarely reach BUILD, and the ceiling is often set by what could be
researched rather than by the quality of the idea. Say so when that is the case, rather than
letting TEST read as a judgement on the idea when it is really a statement about the evidence
available. A founder who returns with five signed pilots has earned a re-audit that can reach
BUILD.

**NARROW** — the idea is real but too broad to execute.
The economics work for a specific segment and not for the general case, or the reachable market
is much smaller than the described one but genuinely reachable. The recommendation names the
segment precisely. This is a common and good outcome: a narrowed idea is usually a better idea.

**TEST** — plausible, but resting on an unverified critical assumption.
The most common honest verdict. The model works if the top assumption holds and collapses if it
does not, and that assumption is cheaply testable. The deliverable is the experiment, not the
product.

**NOT-AS-A-BUSINESS** — the thing is worth making, but not as a source of income.
The arithmetic does not clear the founder's opportunity cost at any reachable volume, or the
market is too small to support it commercially. It may still be an excellent portfolio piece, open
source project, lead generator, or learning exercise — say which, because this verdict is often
heard as "worthless" when it means something quite different.

**REFRAME** — the insight is real, the business model is wrong.
The founder has found something true about the world and packaged it badly: a product that should
be a service, a service that should be a product, a B2C idea whose money is in B2B, a free tool
whose value is in the data or the audience. This is frequently the highest-value outcome of the
entire audit — and it is also the easiest to deliver as an untested opinion, which is why it
carries an extra requirement: **the reframed version must be costed, not just described.**

Run the model twice — scenario A for the idea as described, scenario B for the reframe — and put
them side by side in section 6 with the inputs that differ. A reframe that has not been through
the arithmetic is a suggestion, and the founder has no way to judge it against the version they
already believed in. Two configs, one comparison table:

| | A: as described | B: reframed |
|---|---|---|
| Price / model | 25/mo subscription | 900 one-time + 150/mo |
| Break-even customers | 140 | 12 |
| Month goal is reached | never in 24 | 10 |
| What changed | — | sold as an operated service to larger stores |

If the reframe does not beat scenario A in the arithmetic, it is not a reframe — reconsider
whether the honest verdict is NOT-AS-A-BUSINESS or STOP.

**STOP** — the evidence is against it in any form the founder could execute.
A blocker with no viable route around it, a market that demonstrably does not pay, an incumbent
giving away the core value free, or economics that cannot close at any achievable price and volume.
Requires at least three concrete, specific facts. "Most startups fail" is not one of them. If STOP
cannot be defended with specifics, the honest verdict is TEST.

## Report structure

Use this order. It puts the conclusion first because a founder reading on their phone should get
the answer in the first fifteen seconds, and everything after that is the founder checking the
work.

---

### 1. Verdict — first line of the report

The verdict, then one sentence of reasoning, then the confidence level and why.

> **TEST** — the economics close only if clinics accept a monthly subscription, and no evidence
> was found either way. Everything else in this idea checks out.
>
> *Confidence: moderate. Competitor and market-size data are sourced; pricing and conversion are
> assumed for lack of local comparables.*

If most of the report rests on `[assumed]` inputs, say so here, in this section, not in a footnote.

### 2. The idea, restated falsifiably

The phase 1 sentence, plus a note on where it differs from what the founder described. If the
restatement surfaced a gap — no named customer, no payment, no alternative displaced — that gap is
the finding and it belongs here.

### 3. What the audit rests on

The ranked assumption table, trimmed to the top five, and the one-sentence statement of what the
idea really turns on.

### 4. Evidence

Findings only, each attached to an assumption it bears on. Competitors and prices, substitutes,
who tried and stopped, demand evidence, bottom-up market count. Include what could not be found
and what was searched for — the holes are part of the picture.

### 5. Local market

Findings from the lens, and only the dimensions where something was actually found. Blockers get
their own bolded subsection, stated as blockers, each paired with the routes around it.

### 6. The arithmetic

Inputs table with tags, then the model output: break-even volume and month, LTV/CAC, payback,
trajectory, sensitivity. Then the opportunity-cost comparison, stated in hours and effective
hourly rate.

### 7. What would have to be true

The precise conditions that would move the verdict up a grade. This is the founder's legitimate
route to disagreement and it converts a debate about optimism into a list of checkable facts.

> To move from TEST to BUILD:
> - At least 5 of the next 15 clinics contacted agree to a paid pilot at ≥$25/month
> - A recurring billing route is confirmed available to a sole trader here
> - Conversion from outreach exceeds 4% across at least 40 contacts

### 8. Kill criteria

Written now, while nobody is invested. Numeric, dated, observable.

> Stop if:
> - Fewer than 3 of 30 contacted clinics agree to a paid pilot by 30 November
> - Monthly churn exceeds 12% across the first 20 customers
> - CAC exceeds $90 after the first 25 acquisitions
> - No recurring payment route is confirmed by end of month 2

### 9. The cheapest disproving experiment

One experiment, aimed at the top-ranked assumption. Budget, deadline, method, and a pass/fail
number set in advance.

> **Tests:** will clinics pay monthly?
> **Method:** a one-page offer with a real payment link, sent to 40 clinics from the association
> directory; take pre-orders at $25/month with the first month charged immediately.
> **Cost:** ~$60 and about 12 hours.
> **Deadline:** 3 weeks.
> **Pass:** ≥4 paid. **Fail:** ≤1 paid. **Ambiguous (2–3):** extend to 40 more before deciding.

Not "build an MVP". The experiment buys information about the riskiest assumption at the lowest
price, and an MVP is usually the most expensive way to learn the same thing.

### 10. Evidence appendix

The claim/value/tag/source/date table from phase 2, so the founder can re-check anything.

---

## Calibration

**Length.** The ten sections are a skeleton, not a quota. Sections 1, 2, 7, 8 and 9 are always
present and are usually short — the verdict is a paragraph, the kill criteria are four bullets.
Sections 4, 5 and 6 are as long as the findings warrant and no longer: an idea with two decisive
blockers needs two, not a tour of all eight local dimensions. A section with nothing that changes
the verdict, the arithmetic, or the experiment gets one line saying nothing significant was found,
or is dropped.

A thorough audit of a complex idea can legitimately run long; what it cannot do is pad. The test
is not word count, it is whether any paragraph could be deleted without losing a fact the founder
would act on.

**Tone.** Direct, specific, unhedged about facts, and respectful of the person. The founder is
paying for the truth in a form they can act on. Neither cheerleading nor condescension is useful,
and a report that sounds pleased with its own severity is as much a failure as one that sounds
pleased with the founder.

**Uncertainty.** State it as a level with a reason, never as a blanket disclaimer. "Confidence is
low on pricing because no local comparable exists" is useful; "of course, nobody can predict the
future" is filler.

**Language.** Write the report in the founder's language. Keep the epistemic tags
(`[sourced]`/`[derived]`/`[assumed]`) in their original form regardless of language so they stay
visually distinct and scannable.

## A note on the hardest verdicts

STOP and NOT-AS-A-BUSINESS are the two that require the most care, because they are the two the
founder will remember.

Deliver them early in the report rather than building up to them — burying a STOP under four pages
of balanced context is a form of cowardice that also wastes the founder's time.

Then give the same effort to what remains. A founder who has spent six months on something needs
the honest verdict, and they also need the answer to *"so what do I do now?"*. That may be the
reframed version of the idea, the narrower segment, the part of the work that transfers to
something else, or simply the clear-eyed statement that this particular direction is closed and
they can stop spending on it. The most valuable audits end with the founder relieved rather than
deflated — not because the news was softened, but because they finally know where they stand.
